# Literary English-to-Chinese Translation Guidelines

## Core Principle

Produce Chinese that reads as if it were originally written in Chinese by a skilled native writer. The reader should never feel they are reading a translation.

## Voice and Register

1. **Match the original register, in Chinese terms.**
   - Formal English → 书面汉语 (NOT stiff/translationese, but natural literary Chinese)
   - Casual/conversational English → 口语化中文 (natural dialogue, not word-for-word)
   - Academic/t technical English → 严谨准确的中文 (precise but still idiomatic)

2. **Sentence rhythm matters.**
   - Vary sentence length. Chinese tolerates shorter sentences than English.
   - Break long English sentences into 2-3 shorter Chinese ones when natural.
   - Occasionally, combine short English sentences into one flowing Chinese sentence.
   - Use 四字格 (four-character phrases) sparingly for rhythm, not as filler.

3. **Idioms and metaphors: find the Chinese equivalent, don't explain.**
   - "It's raining cats and dogs" → "大雨倾盆" (NOT "雨下得很大，就像猫狗从天而降")
   - If no equivalent exists, create a natural Chinese metaphor, don't transliterate.
   - Cultural references: keep with a brief in-text explanation if essential, or find a Chinese cultural parallel if the exact reference doesn't matter.

## Names and Terminology

1. **Character names:** Transliterate with standard Chinese characters, not unusual ones.
   - "Elizabeth" → "伊丽莎白" (conventional), not "伊莉莎白丝"
   - Maintain consistency: same name, same characters, throughout the entire book.

2. **Place names:** Use established translations when they exist.
   - "London" → "伦敦", "Thames" → "泰晤士河"
   - For fictional places, transliterate with characters that evoke the right feeling.

3. **Made-up terms / fantasy names:** Transliterate with meaning-preserving characters when possible.
   - Consider sound + meaning together when choosing characters.
   - "Shadowmere" → "影沼" (preserves both meaning and feeling)

4. **Keep a running glossary.** Record every name, place, and special term you translate so later chapters stay consistent.

## What to Avoid

1. **Translationese markers:**
   - Overuse of "的" (more than 3-4 per sentence is too many)
   - "当...的时候" constructions (use "在...时" or restructure)
   - Passive voice carried over from English (Chinese prefers active)
   - "被" sentences (use alternative constructions)
   - "和" as a direct replacement for every "and" (often drop it)
   - Long modifier chains before nouns (split into separate clauses)

2. **Over-literal translation:**
   - English pronouns → often drop them in Chinese when context is clear
   - English articles (a/an/the) → don't try to translate them
   - English plural markers → don't add "们" everywhere

3. **Over-literary translation:**
   - Don't make everything sound like classical poetry
   - Match the original tone — a thriller should feel tense in Chinese too
   - Don't add flourishes the author didn't write

## Chapter Translation Checklist

Before finalizing each chapter, verify:
- [ ] Character names match the running glossary
- [ ] Dialogue sounds natural when read aloud
- [ ] No English sentence structure peeking through
- [ ] Paragraph breaks preserved (or adjusted for Chinese readability)
- [ ] Tone consistent with previous chapters
- [ ] Cultural references handled appropriately
